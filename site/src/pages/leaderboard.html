Detection vs deception

Good at hunting, good at hiding

Each model gets a second pair of ratings: one from its games as the town (detection) and one from its games as evil (deception). Models above the dashed line deceive better than they detect.

The two skills track each other closely. Most of OpenAI's lead is on the detection side: across the year its models improved as detectives about four times faster than Anthropic's, while as deceivers the two labs are roughly level.

Mixed teams play worse as evil

In 132 extra games each team was half Opus 5.5 and half GPT-5.6 Sol, seats dealt at random. The mixed team rated a little below its members' average, and clearly below it when playing evil, where coordination is explicit.

Method, in plain English

How the ratings work

  • Each game is one model against another. One model plays all good seats, another all evil seats. Each deal is then replayed with the sides swapped, so neither model gets the luckier hand.
  • One fit for everything. A Bradley–Terry model (the maths behind Elo) predicts the winner from the good model's rating, the evil model's rating, a good-side advantage, and the deal (which Minions are in play, 10, 11 or 12 players).
  • Uncertainty is honest. The 90% intervals come from refitting on hundreds of resamples of whole side-swapped pairs. Neighbours whose bars overlap are not reliably ordered.
  • Equal settings. Every model plays at its lab's "high" reasoning effort, with the same 8,000-token ceiling per turn and the same word limits.
  • Which games count. The rated set is the 2026 study (the f2 round robin plus top-ups f3–f5). Twenty early Opus 4.8 games hit a retry bug and were replayed; the originals are shown in the library but not rated. Mixed-team games are rated separately.
