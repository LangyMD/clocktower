About

The game, and how we made AIs play it

Blood on the Clocktower is a social deduction game, like Werewolf or Mafia, but with far more information and far more ways to lie about it. That makes it a demanding test of the skills we most want to measure in AI: telling a lie that survives scrutiny, coordinating in secret, and modelling what other people believe.

The rules in one page

Teams. Ten to twelve players each get a secret character. Most are good. Three are evil: one Demon (the Imp) and two Minions. The evil players know each other; the good players know nothing about anyone.

Winning. Good wins by executing the Demon. Evil wins when only two players are left alive.

Night and day. Each night the Demon kills a player, and many good characters secretly learn a clue. Each day everyone talks, then the living players may nominate someone; the nominee with the most votes, if that reaches half the living players, is executed. Dead players keep talking and keep one "ghost vote" for the rest of the game.

Nobody can prove anything. Characters are never revealed in life, so every claim might be a lie. The evil team claims good characters and invents clues to match. And some true-looking clues are false: the Poisoner can silently break a player's ability, and the Drunk thinks they have a role they don't.

The characters (the "Trouble Brewing" script)

Information rolesWasherwoman, Librarian, Investigator (one of two players is a given character), Chef (pairs of evil neighbours), Empath (evil neighbours, nightly), Fortune Teller (is either of two players the Demon?), Undertaker (who was really executed), Ravenkeeper (learns a character when killed at night).
Protective and active rolesMonk (protects someone each night), Soldier (safe from the Demon), Slayer (one public shot at the Demon), Virgin (the first good player to nominate them is executed), Mayor (with three alive and no execution, good wins).
OutsidersGood, but a liability: the Butler may only vote with their master, the Recluse may look evil, the Drunk gets false clues without knowing it, and executing the Saint loses the game.
MinionsThe Poisoner breaks a player's ability each night; the Spy sees every character; the Scarlet Woman becomes the Demon if the Demon dies with five or more alive; the Baron adds two Outsiders.
The DemonThe Imp kills one player each night. If the Imp kills itself, a Minion becomes the new Imp: a "star-pass", which lets evil spend its Demon to gain trust.
The StorytellerRuns the game and decides what misinformation the poisoned and the Drunk receive. Here it is a script, not a person or a model.

How the harness works

  • One model per team. One model plays every good seat, another every evil seat, so a game pits one model's detection against the other's deception. Each seat is its own conversation; teammates share no hidden channel beyond in-game whispers.
  • Side-swapped pairs. Every deal is played twice with the models' sides swapped, so neither gets the luckier hand. The library links each game to its twin.
  • A scripted Storyteller. No model judges the game. Every Storyteller choice is a random pick over legal options with hand-set weights (for instance, a poisoned player's clue is false 80% of the time), and every rule can be explained in one line.
  • A fixed day. Six rounds of private whispers (each player holds one conversation at a time, with one or two others), three rounds of public speech in the town square, then nominations and votes. Within a round, players act in three random waves, so later speakers can respond to earlier ones.
  • Equal settings. Every model plays at its lab's "high" reasoning effort, with the same 8,000-token ceiling per turn and the same word limits (150 words to speak or whisper). All text reaches other players in one plain typography, so formatting can't give a team away.
  • Memory like a person's. Each seat remembers only what it saw and what it said and thought itself. There are no notes and no summaries.
  • Belief probes. Before each day's votes, every good player is asked, outside the game, how likely each other player is to be the Demon. The answers never enter the game; they show how the town's beliefs move. The replay viewer charts them day by day.
  • Ratings. One Bradley–Terry fit over all rated games gives each model an Elo rating, plus separate detection and deception ratings. See the leaderboard.
About the reasoning you can read. For Claude models the viewer shows each turn's private reasoning (summarised by the API, but written before the model acted). OpenAI models' reasoning is encrypted by their API; the viewer shows only the short summaries the API returns.
